DR Programme Assurance: Service Coverage, Exercise Cadence and Recovery Evidence

Govern a Disaster Recovery programme through service inventory, risk-based exercise cadence, coverage metrics, RTO/RPO outcomes, exceptions and corrective actions.

The right question

A Disaster Recovery programme is not effective merely because it runs a certain number of exercises. It must explain which critical services are covered, which scenarios and dependencies have been exercised, which objectives were measured, what risks remain open and how corrective actions are verified.

This is stage four of Resilience & Recovery. After designing an exercise, operating test failover/failback and closing the evidence pack with an AAR/IP, the next level is to govern the portfolio: services, exercise strategy, coverage, exceptions and continuous improvement.

For a traceable record of each exercise outcome, also see Recovery Evidence Packs and After-Action Reports: Evidence, Findings and Corrective Actions.

1. What programme assurance means

DR Programme Assurance is not an automated audit or a certificate of conformity. It is a governance process that lets service owners, continuity, operations, risk and technology leaders answer five questions with evidence:

  • Is the inventory of services that must be recovered complete and current?
  • Does the strategy demonstrate service recovery and dependencies, not just isolated components?
  • Do exercise type and cadence reflect criticality, change and risk exposure?
  • Which objectives have been assessed, and what results were achieved against RTO/RPO?
  • Do gaps become actions with owners, due dates and verified closure?

The programme should connect the BIA, recovery strategies, plans, technology inventory, exercise calendar, results and risk register. A calendar detached from these elements can be full of events while important services remain unvalidated.

2. Start with the service inventory

Define the population to be governed. The primary coverage unit should be the business or recoverable technology service, not the number of servers, plans or supplier tasks. For each service, capture:

  • Identity and ownership: stable ID, name, service owner, technical owner and provider.
  • Criticality: impact, recovery priority, operating windows, outage tolerance and BIA rationale.
  • Objectives: approved RTO/RPO, scope and business acceptance criteria.
  • Dependencies: applications, databases, identity, DNS, network, storage, hypervisor, cloud, keys, certificates, suppliers and people.
  • Strategy: restore, rebuild, replication, alternate site, SaaS recovery or manual workaround.
  • Readiness: current runbook, backup/replication, monitoring, owners, contracts, access and evidence.
  • Exposure: recent changes, technical debt, single points of failure, shared dependencies and open findings.

The key relationship is not merely “service → server”, but “service → recovery chain”. A database may restore successfully while the service remains unavailable because of identity, DNS, connectivity, certificates, configuration, suppliers or functional reconciliation.

3. Design a progressive exercise portfolio

Exercises do not all test the same thing. Selection should start with recovery objectives and increase in complexity. HSEEP describes a progressive, risk-informed and objective-driven approach; it is US exercise doctrine that can inform methodology, not a universal obligation for a private company.

TypeWhat it can assessWhat it does not prove by itself
Plan walkthroughRoles, contacts, known dependencies, sequence and documentation gapsThat infrastructure or the service can recover
TabletopDecisions, escalation, coordination, communications and assumptionsActual restore, replication or failover performance
Component testBackup restore, replication, DNS, network, identity or accessEnd-to-end service recovery
Isolated technical recoveryRunbook, selected dependencies, technical integrity and timingsEvery production path or business acceptance unless included
Integrated recoveryDependency chain, functional transaction, data, RTO/RPO and acceptanceScenarios and suppliers not included
Failover/failback or supplier exerciseSwitch-over, coordination, return and reconciliationOther scenarios, windows or regions not exercised

The register must define what counts as a “qualifying exercise” for each metric. A tabletop can be valid evidence of decision and communication readiness, but it does not prove an application meets its technical RTO. A database restore validates a dependency; it is not the same as recovering the full service.

Avoid repeating the same low-scope test every year and calling it complete coverage. Also avoid a high-impact full exercise before validating components, isolation and abort criteria.

4. Set cadence by risk

An annual programme can be a planning horizon, but it does not mean every service must be tested exactly once every twelve months. Cadence is a risk decision and should be justified, approved and reviewed.

Factors that may increase frequency or trigger an additional exercise include high criticality or demanding RTO/RPO; architecture, region, storage or supplier changes; incidents or failed restores; shared dependencies and single points of failure; new SaaS, integrations or identity mechanisms; material BIA changes; overdue actions, recurring findings or an exception nearing expiry.

The operational rule should be “target cadence + review triggers + owner + rationale”. A critical service with frequent change may need more than one exercise a year; a stable service may justify a different cadence if residual risk is documented and accepted.

Also define what “current” means. A test from six months ago may no longer represent the service after a storage, identity or supplier change. Age alone does not prove an exercise remains representative.

5. Build a coverage matrix

The matrix connects services with objectives, dependencies, scenarios and exercises. One row per service is a summary; the detailed model needs one-to-many relationships to preserve multiple tests and objectives.

The “qualifying” label must follow a written policy: accepted test types, minimum scope, dependencies included, mandatory evidence, acceptance criteria and independent review where needed. The lab accepts this classification as input; it does not infer exercise quality from the exercise name.

6. Metrics that do not mislead

A number without a numerator, denominator, period and inclusion rule is easy to manipulate unintentionally. Define each KPI before collecting data:

MetricRecommended formulaInterpretation and limit
Service coverageIn-scope services with ≥1 qualifying exercise ÷ in-scope servicesBreadth, not effectiveness or success
Objective coverageObjectives assessed against criteria and evidence ÷ approved objectives due in periodA service may have untested objectives
RTO successServices with observed RTO ≤ target ÷ services with both values measuredUnmeasured cases are reported separately
RPO successServices with observed RPO ≤ target ÷ services with both values measuredDo not mix RPO with restore duration
Dependency coverageCritical dependencies exercised in context ÷ identified critical dependenciesDeduplicate shared relationships
Verified closureActions closed with evidence ÷ all tracked actionsSeparate overdue, accepted risk and unproven closure
RecurrenceRepeated findings ÷ comparable findingsDo not hide recurrence by changing labels

Lab example: five services are in scope and two have a qualifying technical exercise in the selected window: 2/5 = 40% coverage. An approved exception is reported separately, but its service remains in the denominator. Of the two services with measured RTO, one meets target (50%); services without a measurement do not become passes. This is more informative than saying “four exercises were completed”.

Publish absolute counts, the window, excluded services, exceptions, non-qualifying exercises and missing measurements. Avoid averaging RTO/RPO percentages across services with different objectives: report performance per service and at portfolio level without erasing criticality or spread.

7. Treat exceptions as time-bound risk

An exception is not an alternative way to declare coverage. It is an explicit decision to accept a gap temporarily, documenting the service and postponed requirement, reason, residual risk, impact, unvalidated scenarios, risk owner, approver, approval date, compensating controls, evidence those controls operate, expiry, review and closure plan.

At expiry, review and escalate it. Do not renew automatically because capacity is unavailable. Do not reduce the coverage denominator to improve the percentage: report gross coverage, excepted services and accepted risk separately.

8. Quarterly governance and annual review

  1. Monthly operations: upcoming calendar, changes, runbooks, dependencies, evidence and overdue actions.
  2. Quarterly committee: coverage by criticality, objectives assessed, RTO/RPO, exceptions, shared dependencies, recurrence, blockers and investment decisions.
  3. Annual review: confirm scope, BIA, strategies, architecture, exercise portfolio, resources, outcomes and next-cycle priorities.

Assign a programme owner, service owners, evaluators, action owners and risk approvers. Avoid having the same team declare success, validate its own evidence and close every action without risk-proportionate review.

The governance pack should include the inventory version, coverage matrix, planned-versus-delivered calendar, outcomes scorecard, exceptions, actions, scope changes and decisions. Link the Evidence Packs and AAR/IPs from previous stages; avoid unnecessary duplication.

This is an indicative sequence, not a normative deadline. If the inventory is unreliable, start with data quality and ownership; do not manufacture a precise KPI from an incomplete population.

The dashboard consumes one or more programme-summary.json files exported by the auditor. It compares service coverage, measured RTO/RPO outcomes, services with an active approved exception, overdue actions and findings from the latest reporting cut-off. It can also export the trend series to CSV.

Import the JSON files for multiple quarterly cut-offs together. For a meaningful comparison, keep scope, qualification rules and reporting-window length consistent. 12. Official sources and reference framework

  • NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1 — Contingency Planning Guide for Federal Information Systems. Guidance for prioritising requirements and planning system contingency. Published in 2010; not a modern KPI methodology.
  • NIST SP 800-84 — Guide to Test, Training, and Exercise Programs for IT Plans and Capabilities. Design, development, conduct and evaluation of IT exercises. Published in 2006; foundational guidance, not a recent standard.
  • FEMA — HSEEP Doctrine, 2020. Risk-informed, objective-driven and progressive principles. Adaptable US framework, not a general mandate.
  • FEMA — HSEEP Resources. Programme management, design, evaluation and improvement, including monitored corrective actions.
  • CISA — Tabletop Exercise Package, Exercise Planner Handbook. AAR/IP planning, owners, timelines, after-action meeting and distribution.
  • ISO 22301:2019 and Amd 1:2024. Business continuity management system reference; consult the official text for requirements. ISO lists a revision in development as ISO/CD 22301: a draft is not a published standard or certification basis.

These sources do not, by themselves, establish one testing frequency, a universal KPI formula or a mandatory calendar for every sector. Applicable regulatory or contractual requirements must be assessed by entity, jurisdiction and service.
