Recovery Evidence Packs and After-Action Reports: Evidence, Findings and Corrective Actions

How to build an auditable Disaster Recovery exercise record: evidence registers, chronology, hashes, findings, recommendations and corrective-action tracking.

Core principle

The evidence pack and the AAR/IP are different products. One helps reviewers locate and assess original artefacts; the other explains what was evaluated, what happened, what it means and what must improve. SHA-256 can help detect a file change, but it does not by itself prove authorship, creation time or chain of custody.

This is stage three of Resilience & Recovery. After designing and measuring the DR exercise and operating test failover or failback, the cycle needs a defensible record. The question is no longer only “did the service recover?” but “what evidence supports that conclusion, what limitations existed and who will correct the gaps?”.

1. Evidence Pack and AAR/IP are different

An evidence pack references the artefacts supporting an evaluation: exported logs, timelines, functional results, change records, metrics, screenshots and approvals. The After-Action Report / Improvement Plan summarises objectives, analyses performance and turns improvement opportunities into actions with owners and due dates.

ProductQuestionContent
Evidence PackWhat happened and which record supports it?Evidence register, originals or controlled references, timeline, metadata, hashes, classification and approvals.
AARWhat did we evaluate and what did we observe?Scope, scenario, objectives, criteria, results, strengths, findings, impact and limitations.
Improvement PlanWhat changes, who owns it and how is it verified?Corrective action, priority, owner, date, dependencies, verification method, status and closure evidence.

A screenshot in a report does not replace the original record and its context. Likewise, a folder containing hundreds of logs is not an analysis of results.

2. Design the evidence register before the exercise

Assign a unique ID to every item and link it to the objective it supports. At minimum record:

  • ID: EV-001, never reused across exercises.
  • Source: system, tool, component or repository that produced it.
  • Description: what it represents, query/filter applied and limitations.
  • Time: event time, export/collection time and ingestion time when available.
  • Timezone: ISO-8601 with explicit offset and relevant time source.
  • Custodian: collector role and independent reviewer role.
  • Relationship: objective, milestone, acceptance criterion, observation and action.
  • Protection: classification, location, retention, owner and access restrictions.

The register should answer which source produced the data, which interval it covers, which filters were applied, who collected it, when, where the original is held and which objective it helps evaluate.

3. Collect without altering the original

Plan which records may expire and how to export them. A DR exercise can involve platform logs, hypervisor events, replication, firewall, DNS, load balancers, databases, middleware, applications, monitoring and business acceptance.

  1. Identify the source: system, environment, component, version and owner.
  2. Preserve the original: export without editing. If you anonymise or crop, retain the original under restricted access and document the derivative.
  3. Record context: interval, filters, query, timezone, tool version, change ID and collecting role.
  4. Assign an EV-ID: link it to the timeline, objective and finding. Avoid ambiguous names such as final2_ok.png.
  5. Calculate integrity: fingerprint the exported file and retain algorithm and value.
  6. Review: a second role confirms the artefact matches its reference, opens correctly and contains no out-of-scope information.

A screenshot adds context but may omit filters, time range, pagination or earlier data. Keep the original export or a reproducible query whenever possible. Do not alter logs to make them easier to read; create a derivative and record the transformation.

4. Timeline: preserve event time and collection time

Preserve at least three time references:

  • Event time: when the source says the activity occurred.
  • Collection time: when the record was exported or collected.
  • Ingestion time: when the central system received or indexed it, if available.

Use ISO-8601 with an explicit offset, such as 2026-11-19T09:34:12+01:00. You may normalise an analytical view to UTC, but preserve the original value and offset. Document NTP, clock drift, adjustments, ingestion delays and differences between machine and operator time. A timestamp in a CSV is not trustworthy unless you know which clock produced it.

The timeline should reference EV-IDs: time, event, component, role, result and deviation. Add request ID, transaction ID, execution ID or change ID when available and safe to retain.

5. Hashes, signatures and storage have different guarantees

SHA-256 produces a fingerprint of the content read. If recalculation later produces a different result, the file no longer matches the original fingerprint. This can detect changes within a controlled workflow.

A standalone hash does not prove who generated the file, whether the source was authentic, whether the time is correct, whether the artefact had already been changed before hashing, or whether the process meets legal or forensic chain-of-custody requirements. It also does not prevent someone replacing both the file and the manifest.

Stronger controls may require a digitally signed manifest, trusted timestamping, immutable/WORM storage, RBAC, access auditing, versioning and independent review. The need depends on risk, retention policy, jurisdiction and incident-response process. Do not call a ZIP with hashes an “evidence vault”.

6. Structure a reviewable folder

DR-EX-2026-017/
├── 00-governance/
│   ├── exercise-charter.pdf
│   ├── approvals/
│   └── participant-roles.csv
├── 01-timeline/
│   ├── master-timeline.csv
│   └── clock-sources.md
├── 02-platform/
├── 03-network-identity/
├── 04-data-and-replication/
├── 05-service-acceptance/
├── 06-findings/
├── 07-improvement-plan/
└── manifest/
    ├── evidence-register.csv
    ├── manifest.json
    └── manifest.sha256

Folders are categories, not permissions. Enforce access in the repository. The index should let a reviewer move from a finding to its objective and exact supporting artefacts.

7. Evaluate objectives with evidence, not impressions

Retrieve the approved objective and measurable criterion. For every objective record the result — met, partially met, not met or not tested — along with observation, impact, evidence and limitations.

ObjectiveCriterionResultEvidence
Recover the test serviceAll tiers available and synthetic transaction accepted.MetEV-001, EV-003
Demonstrate isolation and traceabilityControls reviewed by two roles and references retained.PartialEV-002: second-review reference missing.

The example is synthetic. A technical outcome can be satisfactory while its documentary demonstration remains incomplete. Do not fill that gap by assuming someone reviewed the control.

8. Write the AAR: observation, analysis and recommendation

A DR AAR can contain an executive summary, exercise profile, objective evaluation, strengths, improvement areas, decisions, residual risk and limitations.

Keep the layers distinct: “The second-review identifier was not recorded” is an observation. “This prevents independent review from being demonstrated” is the impact. “Add a reviewer, time and evidence field” is a recommendation. The concrete corrective action, owner and due date belong in the Improvement Plan.

CISA explicitly says an improvement area should describe the problem without including the recommendation or corrective action, which are documented separately. FEMA HSEEP also separates evaluation from improvement planning. These are US exercise references to adapt, not universal obligations for every organisation.

9. Build a verifiable Improvement Plan

Turn every improvement into a verifiable action unless the risk is formally accepted or a documented reason for no action exists. Record action ID and finding, expected change, priority and rationale, owner, target date, dependencies, status, verification method, success criterion and closure evidence.

“Update the procedure” is insufficient. Better: “Add independent second-operator review to the isolation gate and demonstrate in the next exercise that role, time, result and EV-ID are retained”. The action stays open until the new behaviour is demonstrated; editing a document is not enough to close it.

Review overdue actions, blockers, accepted risks and recurrence. FEMA describes the Improvement Plan as a dynamic document whose corrective actions are continually monitored and implemented.

10. Review, approve and distribute

  • every objective has a result and its criteria were not retrospectively changed;
  • every finding has evidence, impact and an associated objective;
  • every improvement has an action and owner/date or approved risk disposition;
  • closed actions have verification evidence, not only a “done” statement;
  • conclusions do not exceed what the sources support;
  • missing evidence, simulations, assumptions and limitations are documented;
  • the service owner and operational/risk owners approve the distributed version.

Separate the full technical pack from the executive edition. The latter may hide internal paths, hostnames, IPs, personal data or security details while preserving traceability to the authorised original. Apply data minimisation, classification and corporate retention rules; do not publish logs, screenshots or customer data.

12. Primary sources and templates

  • FEMA — HSEEP Improvement Planning Templates. AAR/IP templates; corrective-action programmes are described as dynamic documents that are continuously monitored and implemented.
  • CISA — Tabletop Exercise Package: AAR/IP Template. Separates objectives, strengths, improvement areas, analysis, recommendations and Improvement Plan actions.
  • CISA — Emergency Services Sector AAR/IP Template. Sector-specific example of objective evaluation and improvement planning.
  • NIST SP 800-84. Collection of exercise notes/evidence and post-exercise reporting. Published September 2006; foundational guidance, not a recent standard.
  • NIST SP 800-92. Enterprise log management guidance. Published September 2006; general guidance, not a step-by-step forensic procedure.
